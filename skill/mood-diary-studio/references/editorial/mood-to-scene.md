# Mood-to-Scene Translation

Translate emotion through spatial and behavioral choices, not facial expression alone.

## Decision dimensions

### Posture

- withdrawal: compact posture, softened shoulders, reduced reach;
- anticipation: slight forward lean, gaze toward open space;
- fatigue: supported weight, lowered energy, incomplete action;
- relief: released posture, settled object placement;
- quiet joy: small outward movement, relaxed spacing;
- uncertainty: paused gesture, uneven distance from the next action.

Avoid theatrical body language unless the source clearly supports it.

### Space

- isolation: separated relationships and a breathing zone whose distance is
  visually legible;
- possibility: open space ahead of the gaze;
- pressure: reduced breathing room without filling every surface;
- lingering thought: a faint echo near the edge rather than a second full scene;
- recovery: stable horizontal composition, clear hierarchy, and a calm
  breathing zone.

### Motion

Use one restrained motion cue when helpful: a curtain edge, loose page, ribbon, drifting steam, shadow direction, or weather trace. Do not stack motion symbols.

### Character-environment fit

Apply approved silhouette, proportion, and limb geometry before choosing
furniture, platforms, or other support surfaces. Treat those identity anchors
as composition constraints and check:

- where the character's feet, body, or other load-bearing parts contact the
  ground or support;
- whether furniture and support surfaces are scaled to the character rather
  than assumed human-sized;
- whether any occlusion is explained by the action or support while enough
  geometry remains readable; and
- whether the character's lower body and limbs remain accounted for instead of
  disappearing without a reason.

For compact or short mascots, prefer a ground-level relationship or low seat by
default. Use a tabletop, high perch, or human-scale furniture only when the
entry supplies a narrative reason, and state that reason in the composition.

### Object relationships

Prefer distance, orientation, and interruption over extra props. An untouched cup placed beyond reach may communicate more than several literal emotion icons.

Scene objects that imply activity, place, cause, or completion must be
source-supported or explicitly approved. Do not create a document, checklist,
tool, package, or other proxy object to represent an event whose content is
unknown.

## Cultural specificity and provenance

Treat culturally specific clothing, patterns, ceremonies, symbols, and factual
labels tied to a real community as facts that require `user-provided` or a
reliable authorized source. Do not combine unrelated cultural cues into generic
decorative styling. If the source cannot be confirmed, lower the specificity
to non-claiming, broadly descriptive cues rather than guessing.

If a factual displayed label conflicts with a reliable source, report the
conflict and preserve the supplied label as unresolved; do not silently rewrite
or omit it. Request approval before changing the label or its representation.

## Editorial staging boundary

Allow an illustrative environment such as natural light, a curtain, distant
sky, plant shadow, spatial threshold, or layered everyday setting when it:

- carries only a known emotion, time quality, distance, or expectation;
- is marked `editorially constructed` or `inferred` in the brief rather than
  reported as user-provided fact or direct observation;
- remains part of one unified scene and introduces no new event, person,
  specific location, cause, or completion state;
- does not stand in for unknown event content, evidence, or a message source;
- would weaken the emotional relationship if removed.

Do not construct disaster areas, rescue activity, casualties, news devices,
or unobserved event scenes unless reliable source evidence specifically
supports them. Editorial staging may shape an emotional setting; it may not
manufacture the event being felt about.

## Symbolic elements

Use at most one symbolic element under the default visual budget. Keep it subordinate to the lived scene. Reject symbols that merely label the emotion, such as a large broken heart for disappointment or a trophy for relief.

## Selection test

Keep a visual choice only when removing it would weaken the emotional relationship. Otherwise omit it.
